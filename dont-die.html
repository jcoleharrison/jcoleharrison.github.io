01 — SummaryThe one-paragraph truth

Blueprint is the most thoroughly documented self-experiment in the history of longevity — and its most defensible conclusions are unglamorous. After roughly $2M/year, 100+ daily pills, and a decade of exotic interventions, Bryan Johnson himself now points at sleep, whole-food nutrition, daily movement, avoiding harm, and relationships as the bulk of the value [1]. Those levers have genuine human evidence behind them. The novel and expensive parts of the protocol have not been shown to extend human lifespan — and two of its most cinematic experiments (young-plasma exchange and rapamycin) were quietly abandoned as ineffective or harmful [9][10]. Blueprint is an n = 1 case study, not a clinical trial. It is best read as a rigorous demonstration that the basics, done with obsessive consistency, produce excellent biomarkers — not as proof that any pill, drip, or drug buys extra years.

02 — BackgroundWho is Bryan Johnson

Born 1977 in Utah, raised Mormon (later left the church). He founded the payments company Braintree in 2007; Braintree acquired Venmo in 2012, and PayPal bought Braintree in 2013 for ~$800M, netting Johnson an estimated ~$300M [2]. He later founded OS Fund (deep-tech investing) and Kernel (brain-computer interfaces).

Around 2020–2021, after a period of depression, weight gain, and burnout, he launched Project Blueprint: an attempt to run his body like a data-driven system, spending a reported ~$2M/year and tracking 100+ biomarkers with a team of physicians. The 2025 Netflix documentary Don't Die: The Man Who Wants to Live Forever (dir. Chris Smith) brought it to a mass audience [3]. He now also sells Blueprint-branded supplements and foods — a fact that matters when weighing his claims (see §09).

03 — Idea"Don't Die" as an idea

"Don't Die" is the philosophy wrapped around the protocol. Its moves, stated plainly:

  • Not dying is the goal, and the prerequisite for every other goal. Johnson frames longevity as the "zeroth principle" — you can't pursue anything if you're dead — and pitches it as a secular, quasi-religious ethic and a community/movement, not just a routine.
  • "Demote the conscious mind." His central psychological claim is that the deliberate, willpower-driven mind is a poor manager of long-term health (it rationalizes the late-night snack). So he hands decisions to a measured algorithm — data and pre-commitment decide, not in-the-moment desire [3].
  • Measurement over opinion. Every intervention is supposed to earn its place by moving a biomarker. In practice this ideal is compromised by n = 1 sampling and conflicts of interest (§09), but the principle — act on data, not vibes — is sound.

Note the epistemic status: the philosophy is a set of values and framing devices. It is neither true nor false in the scientific sense — it's a motivational operating system. Judge it on whether it produces adherence (it clearly does for him), not on whether it's "proven."

04 — MethodHow to read the grades

Every factual claim in this document carries one of four grades. The grade describes the strength of human evidence for the claim as stated — not whether Johnson believes it.

EstablishedRobust human evidence and broad scientific consensus. Safe to act on.
PromisingPlausible mechanism + some human data, but not proven to extend lifespan/healthspan.
AnecdotalA single-person self-report (n = 1). Real measurement, zero generalizability.
Dropped / RefutedAbandoned by Johnson, or contradicted by better evidence.

The one caveat that governs everything

No intervention — not one — has ever been shown to extend maximum human lifespan. Every longevity claim below is really a claim about risk factors, biomarkers, or animal models standing in for lifespan, because a direct human lifespan trial is infeasible. Keep that substitution in mind whenever you read "anti-aging."

05 — SystemThe protocol, by system

Blueprint is best understood as five stacked layers. The layers are ordered here by how much evidence supports them — which is roughly the opposite of how much attention they get.

Sleep — the top lever Established

Johnson calls sleep "the most powerful drug in the world" and treats bedtime as the most important appointment of the day. His high-value habits are nearly all free: a fixed bedtime (±30 min every day), a 30–60 min wind-down with no screens, a cool, dark, quiet room, morning light, an early caffeine cutoff, and a last meal hours before bed. He estimates ~80% of his sleep gains came from these free habits, not gadgets [4].

Nutrition — plant-forward, calorie-aware, early Established Promising

A largely vegan, whole-food diet of ~2,000–2,250 kcal/day, eaten in a compressed morning window (all food finished by ~11am–noon — an early time-restricted-eating pattern). No alcohol, minimal added sugar, capped caffeine. The two fixed daily meals are public:

Super Veggie
Black lentils, broccoli, cauliflower, shiitake/maitake mushrooms, garlic, ginger, cumin, hemp seeds, extra-virgin olive oil [5].
Nutty Pudding
Macadamia & walnuts, chia, cocoa, berries, pomegranate juice, sometimes pea protein [5].
Third meal
Varies day to day; same plant-forward template.

The whole-food, plant-forward pattern and the avoidance of alcohol/junk are Established goods. Caloric restriction and time-restricted eating are Promising: the CALERIE RCT showed ~25% calorie reduction improves cardiometabolic markers and modestly slows the pace of aging over 2 years, but human lifespan benefit remains unproven [6].

Exercise — ~1 hr/day Established

Roughly 6 hours/week blending resistance training, zone-2 cardio, high-intensity intervals (4×4), plus flexibility and balance, with movement breaks through the day. This is squarely evidence-based: both aerobic fitness (VO₂ max) and strength/lean mass are among the strongest predictors of all-cause mortality we have. Johnson notes even 20 min/day delivers most of the benefit [1].

Supplements & medications — ~100 pills/day Promising

The stack has changed repeatedly (he has been reducing it under a "do less" principle). Recurring core items: vitamin D, omega-3 (EPA/DHA), B12/B-complex, creatine, magnesium, zinc, CoQ10/ubiquinol, NAC, garlic extract, curcumin, taurine, glucosamine, plus a "Longevity Mix" drink. Grades vary by ingredient — see the ledger (§07). The honest summary: a few have solid human data (creatine, omega-3, correcting a vitamin-D deficiency); most rest on mechanism and biomarker studies; none is proven to extend human life.

Measurement & advanced therapies Promising Anecdotal

Measurement: monthly blood panels, DEXA, MRI, ultrasound, colonoscopy, VO₂-max testing, continuous glucose monitoring, WHOOP, and quarterly epigenetic clocks — 100+ markers, some resolved to organ-specific "ages." Rigorous quantified-self, genuinely useful for catching problems early.

Therapies layered on top include red/near-infrared light and hyperbaric oxygen (HBOT). These have some supportive data for narrow endpoints (skin, wound healing, inflammation markers) but weak evidence for slowing aging. The more experimental additions — and the ones that failed — are covered in §08.

08 — Negative resultsWhat was tried and dropped

This is arguably the most valuable output of Blueprint, because negative results rarely get published in the wellness world. The pattern is stark: the most futuristic interventions are the ones that failed.

  • Rapamycin Dropped — a leading "longevity drug" candidate. After roughly five years, Johnson stopped, citing recurrent skin/soft-tissue infections, lipid and glucose abnormalities, and elevated resting heart rate; a preprint then reported rapamycin was associated with accelerated aging across epigenetic clocks in humans [9].
  • Young-plasma exchange Dropped — the headline experiment from the documentary (Bryan received his father's plasma; his son gave to Bryan). He reported no measurable benefit and abandoned it, later acknowledging any apparent effect on his father may have been from removing "old" plasma, not the transfer itself [10].
  • Growth hormone Dropped — stopped after ~100 days over side effects including raised intracranial pressure and elevated blood glucose.
  • Follistatin gene therapy Anecdotal — an experimental, unregulated gene therapy taken abroad, with self-reported gains. Highly speculative; not validated.

The lesson

When a well-funded, closely-measured person runs the sci-fi interventions on himself and drops them, that is a result. It tells you the exotic longevity market is running well ahead of its evidence — and that the durable wins came from the boring layers.

09 — CaveatsConflicts & things to keep in mind

  • It's a case study, not a trial. n = 1, no control, self-published. You cannot separate the effect of any single intervention from the ~100 others, from his wealth, or from regression to the mean after an unhealthy baseline.
  • He sells what he studies. Blueprint markets supplements, foods, olive oil, and protein powder derived from the protocol — a direct commercial incentive attached to the health claims [2].
  • Product-quality flags. Independent, community-funded third-party testing and California Prop 65 notices reported detectable lead, cadmium, and/or arsenic in several Blueprint products, despite purity marketing (olive oil was a notable exception) [11]. "Third-party tested" is a claim to verify, not to trust by default.
  • Scoring conflict. The Rejuvenation Olympics leaderboard he tops is run with TruDiagnostic, the company selling the epigenetic tests — the referee and the vendor overlap [7].
  • Non-science controversies. There have been public disputes with former employees/associates (e.g. allegations by ex-fiancée and former employee Taryn Southern regarding an NDA and employment). These are allegations and are separate from the science, but they bear on how much benefit of the doubt his self-reported data earns.

11 — PracticeThe free 90%

If you want the evidence-backed core of Blueprint at ~0% of the cost, this is it — and it's essentially what Johnson himself now recommends first [1]:

  • Sleep: same bedtime daily (±30 min), cool dark room, screens off and caffeine cut early, wind-down ritual.
  • Move: aim for ~150+ min/week aerobic + 2–3 strength sessions; even 20 min/day helps. Build VO₂ max and keep muscle.
  • Eat: mostly whole plants, adequate protein, minimal ultra-processed food and added sugar; don't overeat; consider an earlier eating window.
  • Don't add harm: no smoking/vaping, little to no alcohol, less doomscrolling.
  • Connect: invest in relationships and community — a real longevity variable, not a soft one.
  • Basics-first supplements (optional): creatine, omega-3, and vitamin D if a test shows you're low. Skip the exotic stack until the basics are locked in.
  • Measure lightly: a periodic blood panel and knowing your key numbers beats an expensive gadget you ignore.

12 — FrontierOpen questions worth watching

  • Does lowering an epigenetic clock causally extend healthy life, or only track it? (The field's central open question.)
  • Will any drug candidate (e.g. from the CR/mTOR/senolytic families) show a hard human outcome in a controlled trial?
  • How much of Blueprint's biomarker success is the protocol vs. simply going from an unhealthy baseline to disciplined basics?
  • Can rigorous n = 1 self-experimentation, aggregated across many people (the "Olympics" model), ever substitute for RCTs? Probably not for causation — but it may generate hypotheses.
