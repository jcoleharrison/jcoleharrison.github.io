01 — SummaryThe one-paragraph truth

Blueprint is the most thoroughly documented self-experiment in the history of longevity — and its most defensible conclusions are unglamorous. After a reported ~$2M/year, 100+ daily pills, and a decade of exotic interventions, Bryan Johnson himself now leads with sleep, whole-food nutrition, daily movement, avoiding harm, and relationships [1]. Those levers have genuine human evidence. The novel and expensive parts have not been shown to extend human lifespan — and the most cinematic experiments were abandoned: young-plasma exchange (no measurable benefit) and rapamycin (dropped in 2024 after ~5 years, citing side effects) [10][11]. Blueprint is an n = 1 case study, not a clinical trial. Read it as a rigorous demonstration that the basics, done with obsessive consistency, produce elite biomarkers — not as proof that any pill, drip, or drug buys extra years.

02 — BackgroundWho is Bryan Johnson

Born 1977 in Utah, raised Mormon (later left the church). He founded the payments company Braintree (2007); Braintree acquired Venmo (2012), and PayPal bought Braintree in 2013 for ~$800M, netting Johnson an estimated ~$300M [2]. He later started OS Fund (deep-tech investing) and Kernel (brain–computer interfaces).

Around 2020–2021, after a period of depression, weight gain, and burnout, he launched Project Blueprint — running his body as a data-driven system, reportedly investing ~$25M of his own capital to build it and spending ~$2M/year to run it, tracking 100+ biomarkers with a physician team [3]. The 2025 Netflix film Don't Die: The Man Who Wants to Live Forever (dir. Chris Smith) brought it mainstream [4]. Blueprint is now also a commercial brand selling supplements and food — a fact that shapes how to read his claims (see §11).

03 — Idea"Don't Die" as an idea

"Don't Die" is a philosophy wrapped around the protocol — and Johnson is explicit that he intends it as something bigger than a routine. Its moves, stated plainly:

  • Not dying is the zeroth principle. You can't pursue any other goal if you're dead, so longevity comes first. He frames this as a secular, quasi-religious ethic and a community — there's a self-published book (Don't Die, credited to "Zero"), and ticketed Don't Die summits (SF 2024, then Singapore, LA, NYC) [5].
  • "Demote the conscious mind." His central psychological claim: the willpower-driven mind is a poor manager of long-term health, so hand decisions to a measured algorithm. He describes outsourcing the body to an algorithm as the ultimate form of human–AI "alignment" [5].
  • It's pitched as an AI-era moral framework. Johnson argues a "self-terminating" species can't safely build superintelligence, and positions "Don't Die" as an ideology aligning humans and machines around preserving existence. Whether that's visionary or grandiose is a values question — not a scientific one.
  • Measurement over opinion. Every intervention is meant to earn its place by moving a biomarker. The principle is sound; in practice it's compromised by n = 1 sampling and conflicts of interest (§11).

Epistemic status: the philosophy is a set of values and framing devices — a motivational operating system, neither true nor false in the scientific sense. Judge it on whether it produces adherence (it clearly does for him), not on whether it's "proven."

04 — MethodHow to read the grades

Every factual claim here carries one of four grades describing the strength of human evidence for the claim as stated — not whether Johnson believes it, and not whether the idea is interesting.

EstablishedRobust human evidence and broad consensus. Safe to act on.
PromisingPlausible mechanism + some human data, but not proven to extend lifespan/healthspan.
AnecdotalSingle-person self-report (n = 1). Real measurement, zero generalizability.
Dropped / RefutedAbandoned by Johnson, or contradicted by better evidence.

The caveat that governs everything

No intervention — not one — has ever been shown to extend maximum human lifespan. Every longevity claim below is really about risk factors, biomarkers, or animal models standing in for lifespan, because a direct human lifespan trial is infeasible. Hold that substitution in mind whenever you read "anti-aging."

Illustrative — strength of human evidence per layer, not a measured quantity. Public attention runs in the reverse order.

Sleep — the top lever Established

Johnson calls sleep "the most powerful drug in the world" and treats bedtime as the most important appointment of the day. The high-value habits are nearly all free: a fixed bedtime (±30 min daily), a 30–60 min screen-free wind-down, a cool, dark, quiet room, morning light, an early caffeine cutoff, and a last meal hours before bed. He estimates ~80% of his sleep gains came from these free habits, not gadgets [6].

Nutrition — plant-forward, calorie-aware, early Established Promising

A largely vegan, whole-food diet of ~2,000–2,250 kcal/day, eaten in a compressed morning window (all food finished by ~11am–noon — early time-restricted eating). No alcohol, minimal added sugar, capped caffeine. Two fixed daily meals are public:

Super Veggie
Black lentils, broccoli, cauliflower, shiitake/maitake mushrooms, garlic, ginger, cumin, hemp seeds, extra-virgin olive oil [7].
Nutty Pudding
Macadamia & walnuts, chia, cocoa, berries, pomegranate juice, sometimes pea protein [7].
Third meal
Varies daily; same plant-forward template.

The whole-food, plant-forward pattern and avoidance of alcohol/junk are Established goods. Caloric restriction and time-restricted eating are Promising — the strongest human data is the CALERIE-2 RCT (§08).

Exercise — ~1 hr/day, 6 hr/week Established

Resistance training, zone-2 cardio, high-intensity intervals (4×4), plus flexibility and balance, with movement breaks through the day. Squarely evidence-based: cardiorespiratory fitness (VO₂ max) and muscle strength are among the strongest predictors of all-cause mortality we have. Johnson notes even 20 min/day delivers most of the benefit [1].

Supplements & medications — ~100 pills/day Promising

The stack changes often (he's been reducing it under a "do less" principle). Recurring items: vitamin D, omega-3 (EPA/DHA), B12/B-complex, creatine, magnesium, zinc, CoQ10/ubiquinol, NAC, garlic extract, curcumin, taurine, glucosamine, plus a "Longevity Mix." Grades vary by ingredient (§08). Honest summary: a few have solid human data (creatine, omega-3, correcting a vitamin-D deficiency); most rest on mechanism and biomarkers; none is proven to extend human life.

Measurement & therapies Promising Anecdotal

Measurement: monthly blood panels, DEXA, MRI, ultrasound, colonoscopy, VO₂-max testing, continuous glucose, WHOOP, quarterly epigenetic clocks — 100+ markers, some resolved to organ-specific "ages." Rigorous quantified-self, genuinely useful for catching problems early. Therapies layered on include red/near-infrared light and hyperbaric oxygen (HBOT): some support for narrow endpoints (skin, wound healing, inflammation markers), weak evidence for slowing aging. The experimental additions — and the ones that failed — are in §09.

06 — InstrumentsThe epigenetic-clock question

Almost every dramatic Blueprint headline ("aging at 0.64", "biologically younger") comes from an epigenetic clock — an algorithm that reads DNA-methylation patterns and outputs an age or a rate. Understanding what these do and don't measure is the single biggest key to reading the whole protocol honestly.

  • Generations of clocks. First-gen (Horvath, Hannum) predict chronological age. Second-gen (PhenoAge, GrimAge/GrimAge2) are trained on mortality and disease, so they track health risk better. DunedinPACE is different again: built from the Dunedin cohort's measured rate of physiological decline across 19 organ-system biomarkers, it estimates your current pace of aging — a speedometer, not an odometer [8].
  • What's genuinely validated. DunedinPACE has been replicated in 50+ cohorts across many countries and ancestries, shows good test-retest reliability, and predicts morbidity, disability, and mortality. As a predictor, it's real science [8].
  • Where the claims outrun the data. Clocks are statistical models; the methylation sites they use don't necessarily reflect a causal aging mechanism, so a lower score is a better forecast, not proof you've repaired damage. Different clocks measure different things and disagree, and a formal analysis showed clock "noise" is large enough to challenge the reliability of the rejuvenation effects people report [9]. Crucially: that lowering a clock causally adds healthy years is a hypothesis under active study, not an established fact.

The rule of thumb

Treat an epigenetic-clock reading like a credit score for aging: useful, validated against real outcomes, and worth improving — but the number is a proxy, and "I lowered my score" is not the same claim as "I added years to my life."

09 — Negative resultsTried and dropped

Arguably Blueprint's most valuable output — negative results rarely surface in the wellness world. The pattern is stark: the most futuristic interventions are the ones that failed.

  • Rapamycin Dropped — after ~5 years, Johnson stopped in 2024, citing recurrent skin/soft-tissue infections, lipid and glucose changes, and elevated resting heart rate, plus a preprint reporting rapamycin was associated with accelerated aging across 16 epigenetic clocks. Important nuance: that's a single preprint, and clocks may simply fail to capture rapamycin's benefits — rapamycin remains the most reproducible lifespan-extending drug in animals (§10) [11].
  • Young-plasma exchange Dropped — the documentary's headline experiment (Bryan received his father's plasma; his son gave to Bryan). No measurable benefit; abandoned. The mainstream science suggests parabiosis effects in animals come mostly from diluting factors in old blood, not from anything special in young blood [10].
  • Growth hormone Dropped — stopped after ~100 days over side effects including raised intracranial pressure and elevated blood glucose.
  • Follistatin gene therapy Experimental — a single Minicircle injection in a Honduran special economic zone (Sept 2023, ~$25k) explicitly to bypass US regulation. Self-reported muscle and biomarker gains; no controlled evidence, unknown long-term safety [20].

The lesson

When a well-funded, closely-measured person runs the sci-fi interventions on himself and drops them, that is a result. It says the exotic longevity market is running well ahead of its evidence — and that the durable wins came from the boring layers.

10 — ReceptionWhat the experts say

The scientific reception is a useful triangulation — broadly: the basics are right, the framing is oversold, and it's a case study, not evidence.

  • Peter Attia (longevity physician) praises the data-driven approach and the emphasis on cardiovascular fitness, but cautions that more interventions ≠ better outcomes and that lifestyle basics cover most of the longevity gains for typical people [13].
  • Matt Kaeberlein (aging biologist, rapamycin researcher) is blunt that Johnson "is not a credible voice" and called the rapamycin exit more publicity than science — while defending rapamycin itself as the most robust pharmacological lifespan intervention known in animals, and noting a single case can't prove a drug ineffective [11].
  • Independent science reviews (e.g. the American Council on Science and Health) frame Blueprint as "longevity or marketing?" — acknowledging the basics while stressing that the reversal claims are, at best, an n = 1 case study dressed as a study [21].
  • The field polices itself in real time. The taurine reversal (2023 hype → 2025 rebuttal) and the rapamycin-clock debate are reminders that individual longevity findings are provisional; building a fixed protocol on the newest paper is risky by design [17].

11 — CaveatsConflicts & controversies

  • It's a case study, not a trial. n = 1, no control, self-published. You can't separate any single intervention from the ~100 others, from his wealth, or from regression to the mean after an unhealthy baseline.
  • He sells what he studies. Blueprint is now a commercial brand (supplements, food, subscriptions); it raised a $60M round in Oct 2025, and in Feb 2026 launched "Immortals," offering his protocol to a few clients at $1M/year each [3]. Commercial incentives are attached to the health claims.
  • Product-quality dispute. An Environmental Research Center Prop 65 notice (Feb 2024) and independent community-funded testing reported detectable lead, cadmium, and/or arsenic in several Blueprint products (cocoa powder, matcha, Longevity Mix) despite "free of contaminants" marketing. Blueprint says it spent $600k+ on testing, publishes COAs, and that independent results were within margin of error of its own [22]. Fair reading: trace heavy metals are near-ubiquitous in cocoa and plant powders, and Prop 65 is a strict labeling threshold, not proof of harm — the real issue is the purity marketing versus detectable presence. Verify COAs; don't assume.
  • Scoring conflict. The Rejuvenation Olympics leaderboard he tops is run with TruDiagnostic, the company selling the epigenetic tests — referee and vendor overlap [10].
  • Labor & conduct allegations. A 2025 New York Times investigation reported Johnson's long-running use of NDAs with employees, partners, and vendors, and users reporting side effects from the Longevity Mix; some test subjects said the protocol made them feel unwell. Johnson pre-emptively called it a "hit piece" and afterward said it "missed." These are allegations, separate from the science — but they bear on how much benefit of the doubt the self-reported data earns [23].

13 — PracticeThe free 90%

The evidence-backed core of Blueprint at ~0% of the cost — essentially what Johnson himself now recommends first [1]:

  • Sleep: same bedtime daily (±30 min), cool dark room, screens off and caffeine cut early, wind-down ritual.
  • Move: ~150–180 min/week aerobic + 2–3 strength sessions; build VO₂ max, keep muscle. Even 20 min/day helps.
  • Eat: mostly whole plants, adequate protein, minimal ultra-processed food and added sugar; don't overeat; consider an earlier eating window.
  • Don't add harm: no smoking/vaping, little to no alcohol, less doomscrolling.
  • Connect: invest in relationships and community — a real longevity variable, not a soft one.
  • Basics-first supplements (optional): creatine, omega-3, and vitamin D if a test shows you're low. Skip the exotic stack until the basics are locked in.
  • Measure lightly: a periodic blood panel and knowing your key numbers beats an expensive gadget you ignore.

14 — FrontierOpen questions worth watching

  • Does lowering an epigenetic clock causally extend healthy life, or only track it? (The field's central open question.)
  • Will any drug candidate (rapamycin, metformin/TAME, senolytics) show a hard human outcome in a controlled trial?
  • How much of Blueprint's biomarker success is the protocol vs. simply going from an unhealthy baseline to disciplined basics?
  • Can aggregated n = 1 self-experiments (the "Olympics" model) ever substitute for RCTs? Probably not for causation — but they may generate hypotheses worth testing properly.
